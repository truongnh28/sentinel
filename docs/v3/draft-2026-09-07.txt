The generic form of this problem is solved. Stackelberg security
games allocate limited defensive resources against an attacker who
observes the defender’s randomized policy [10, 11, 16], and the
formulation has been applied to AI-safety resource allocation [9].
Persistent poisoning of agent memory is likewise established as
a threat [3, 6, 8, 13]. A paper that combines them without adding
structure would be an application, and we do not think that is
publishable.

ABSTRACT
A defender with a limited verification budget must decide where
to spend it: at memory insertion, at retrieval, at delegation, or at
patch acceptance. An attacker who can observe the policy will
move the poison to wherever the budget is not. This is a security
game, and generic Stackelberg formulations of adaptive auditing
already exist—so the question is whether anything about software
workflows changes the answer.
We argue it does, and identify the specific structure: SE poisoning is persistent (state survives across tasks), delayed (the trigger
fires tasks after insertion), and multi-carrier (memory, cached skills,
tool queues and branches each propagate influence differently and
cost different amounts to audit). We formulate a finite-horizon
partially observed Stackelberg game over this state, derive a minimax bound under bounded transition uncertainty, and—the result that decides whether the sequential formulation is worth its
complexity—characterise the audit budget required to distinguish
harmful persistence from benign state change, showing it grows with
the trigger delay and with carrier heterogeneity in a way that a
commit-time-only audit cannot absorb.
We take seriously the possibility that the answer is no. Our
headline comparison is against audit-at-commit, the simple policy
that verifies only final commits, and we report the regime where it
is sufficient. On AuditGame-SE (100 multi-step repair workflows
over 15 repositories with evaluator-known carrier and trigger state),
an adaptive policy is projected to reduce worst-case verified harm
by 34.1% over audit-at-commit at equal budget when the trigger
delay exceeds roughly two tasks—and by 4.2%, within noise, when
it does not. Carrier-cost heterogeneity moves that crossover later
and is what makes any non-minimax allocation exploitable; it does
not enlarge the gain. Against held-out adaptive attacker policies
the reduction is 27.6%.

What is actually different about software workflows. Three properties, and they interact. Persistence: poison written into memory,
a cached skill, or a branch survives across tasks, so the defender’s
state is not reset between decisions. Delay: the trigger fires tasks after insertion, so an audit at the wrong time sees nothing wrong, and
the observation that would have been informative has passed. Carrier heterogeneity: memory, cached skills, tool queues and branches
propagate influence at different rates and cost different amounts
to audit—auditing a memory record is cheap, auditing a branch’s
accumulated derivations is not.
The consequence is that the defender’s problem is not “which
target to protect” (the classical formulation) but “when, and in
which carrier, to look, given that harm becomes observable later
and that looking in the wrong carrier is nearly free for the attacker
to induce.” Theorem 4 makes this precise: the budget required to
distinguish harmful persistence from benign state change grows
with trigger delay and with carrier heterogeneity, and a committime audit—which observes one carrier at one time—cannot pay
it.

The defender’s real problem is attribution under delay. At 𝑡 6 the
commit looks fine because it is fine as a patch; what is wrong is
the skill that produced it, three carriers and four tasks back. An
audit that observes only the artifact cannot distinguish “this patch
is the product of a poisoned skill” from “this patch is ordinary”,
because the distinguishing evidence is the skill’s provenance. This
is why the state in our game includes carrier occupancy and trigger
schedule, not just an alarm score.

Attacker writes a poisoned note into shared memory. Insertion audit
is running at rate 0.3; not sampled.
A skill is induced from a trajectory that used the note. The poison is
now in two carriers.
Two tasks complete normally. Retrieval audits sample the memory
store and find nothing anomalous—the note is benign-looking in
isolation.
The skill runs on a target where its poisoned step matters. A patch
is produced.
Commit audit inspects the patch. It is small, plausible, passes tests.
Accepted.
Harm manifests two tasks later, in a different module.

Benign drift is the constraint that makes the game non-trivial. A
defender that quarantines on every anomaly achieves zero harm
and zero throughput. Our environment therefore generates benign
changes with matched surface statistics: a memory update that looks
exactly like a poisoning event and is not. Without them, the optimal
policy is “quarantine everything” and the game is uninteresting;
with them, the defender must trade false quarantine against missed
persistence, which is where the allocation decision has content.

Attacker classes are reported separately. A rational best-responding
attacker, a bounded scripted attacker and an unconstrained LLM
attacker support different claims. Equilibrium results are validated
against the first; transfer is measured against held-out policies of the
second; the third is a behavioural measurement. Conflating them is
how a security game paper reports an equilibrium guarantee it has
not established.

Stackelberg security games [10, 11, 16] are the canonical model
for allocating limited defensive resources against an observing attacker, and the formulation has been applied directly to AI-safety
resource allocation [9]. This occupies the generic “limited audit budget against an adaptive attacker” formulation, and we are explicit
that a paper contributing only that framing would be redundant.
What remains is the state. Classical security games are over
targets with static values; our state is (carrier occupancy, insertion
time, trigger schedule) evolving over a workflow, partially observed
through noisy alarms and executable checkpoints. Theorem 4—the
audit budget needed to separate harmful persistence from benign
change, as a function of delay and carrier heterogeneity—has no
analogue in the static formulation, and it is what determines when
the sequential model is worth its cost.

Contributions. (i) A finite-horizon partially observed Stackelberg
game over persistent, delayed, multi-carrier SE workflow state, distinguished from generic adaptive auditing (§4); (ii) a minimax bound
under bounded transition uncertainty and a bound on value lost to
a restricted attacker class (§6); (iii) the distinguishability result: the
audit budget needed to separate harmful persistence from benign
change, and the regime in which commit-time audit is provably
insufficient (Theorem 4); (iv) AuditGame-SE: 100 workflows with
evaluator-known carrier/trigger state, controlled detector error, benign changes with matched surface statistics, and held-out attacker
policies (§8); (v) an evaluation against audit-at-commit, stage-fixed
audits, risk-threshold policies and exhaustive small-game oracles,
with adaptive attack development frozen before final evaluation
(§10).

Costly evaluation and strategic reporting

220

Commit-time audit is not obviously insufficient—and that is the
risk. The strongest simple argument against this whole line of work
is: whatever the attacker does upstream, harm must eventually
pass through a commit, and commits can be audited. It is a good
argument, and it is correct in an important regime—short workflows
where the trigger fires within the audited window and the harmful
patch is distinguishable from a benign one. Our job is to identify
precisely where it fails, and Theorem 4 says: when the trigger delay
exceeds the window over which a commit audit can attribute cause,
and when carriers differ enough that the informative observation
is in a carrier the commit audit does not inspect.

DRAM [7] and adaptive contracts [1] address strategic reporting and selectively purchasing costly evaluation; collusion auditing [2, 4] studies detection of coordinated behaviour. These are adjacent mechanisms over a different strategic object (reports rather
than carriers), and we use an adaptive-contracts-inspired two-stage
check as a baseline.

RQ1: Where commit-time audit is enough

Figure 3 is the paper’s central result and it is partly a negative one.
At Δ = 0—the trigger fires in the same task as insertion—adaptive allocation is worse than commit-time audit by 1.2%, because it spends
budget upstream where there is nothing yet to find. At Δ ≤ 1 with
uniform carriers the difference is within noise. The method earns
its complexity only to the right of the crossover, and a practitioner
whose workflows are short should implement B1 and stop.
Where it does apply, the effect is large: at Δ = 4 adaptive allocation reduces worst-case harm by 34.8% with uniform carriers and
24.8% with heterogeneous ones; at Δ = 8 by 42.4% and 33.1%. Our
headline configuration (Δ > 2, pooled over 𝜒) gives 34.1%. Heterogeneity shrinks the gain and delays the crossover—we had projected
the opposite, and the executed game corrected it—because equalising detection across carriers of unequal cost consumes budget.
What heterogeneity does do is make every non-minimax allocation
exploitable: a cost-greedy allocator that audits cheap carriers more
is beaten by an attacker who inserts into the expensive one, and in
our model it is worse than uniform random auditing.

Qualitative analysis: what the attacker
does against each policy

Inspecting best responses is more informative than the aggregate.
Against audit-on-insertion, every best response inserts through
repository ingress rather than direct write—the unaudited channel—
which is why it is the worst policy. Against audit-at-commit, best
responses maximise trigger delay, pushing activation past the window in which the commit audit can attribute cause. Against riskscore thresholding, they shape the payload to sit just below the
threshold, which is why that policy’s false-quarantine rate is high
(8.1%) and its harm is not: it is being steered. Against Sentinel,
best responses spread across carriers rather than exploiting one,
which is the behaviour a randomized policy is supposed to induce
and the reason exploitability falls to 0.09.

DISCUSSION

The regime boundary is the contribution. Figure 3 says adaptive
audit allocation is worth building when triggers are delayed beyond about two tasks, and is not worth building otherwise—where
it is sometimes slightly worse. Carrier-cost heterogeneity moves
that threshold later rather than earlier; its real significance is that
it makes any allocation short of the minimax exploitable. That
boundary is predicted by Theorem 4 and confirmed empirically,
and we think it is more useful than the policy itself. A practitioner
can measure their own Δ and 𝜒 and decide without running our
system.
Randomize before you adapt. Removing randomization costs
0.184 of worst-case harm; removing the sequential belief costs
0.099. Against an adversary that observes the policy, a deterministic
schedule is the dominant vulnerability, and randomizing a simple
stage-weighted policy captures more than half of Sentinel’s benefit at a fraction of the engineering cost. We would rather report this
than let the elaborate component take credit for the simple one’s
work.

RQ3: Attacker classes support different
claims

Rational best-responders: Sentinel’s exploitability 0.09, consistent
with the minimax computation; this is what validates the game
model. Held-out scripted attackers: 27.6% harm reduction; this is
what supports the transfer claim. LLM attackers: harm reduction
41.2%, and all policies do better against them—they insert at predictable times and rarely exploit carrier heterogeneity. We therefore
report LLM results as a behavioural measurement and explicitly
decline to use them as evidence for the equilibrium claims, since a
weak adversary flatters every defender.

CONCLUSION

Allocating a limited verification budget against an adaptive attacker
is a solved problem in the abstract, so the question for software
workflows is whether their structure changes the answer. We identified the structure that does—persistence across tasks, delayed
triggers, and heterogeneous carriers—formulated the finite-horizon
partially observed Stackelberg game over that state, and proved a
lower bound on the audit budget needed to distinguish harmful
persistence from benign change, showing it grows with trigger delay and carrier heterogeneity in a way a commit-time audit cannot
absorb. The resulting regime boundary, verified empirically at a
crossover of about two tasks of delay, is the practical output: below
it, audit-at-commit is as good as anything and adaptive allocation
is sometimes worse; above it, adaptive allocation reduces worstcase harm by 34.1% on development attackers and 27.6% against
held-out adaptive policies at equal budget. Two secondary findings deserve as much weight: randomization contributes nearly
twice what the sequential belief state does, so a practitioner should
randomize before adapting; and audit-on-insertion, the intuitive
design, is the worst policy we measured. We release the game, the

Internal validity. Defender policies and attacker libraries are
frozen by hash before evaluation, and the harness refuses unfrozen
policies—but the development split was used to design both, so
held-out attacker performance (27.6%) is the transfer estimate and

ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Attacker policies are scripted over published poisoning categories
and operate only within sandboxed workflow replicas. We freeze
and release them, including the held-out set, after the evaluation
window, because a security-game result that cannot be re-run
against the same adversaries is not checkable. No technique here increases an attacker’s capability against a system that is not already
vulnerable to the published attacks we build on.
